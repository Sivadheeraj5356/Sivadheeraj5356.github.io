NOTES ON HUMAN-AI INTERACTION · 08 / 10

Show me what you'd do

Asking users to specify what they want is a tax. The best interfaces pay it for them.

Every settings page, every empty prompt, every chipper "how can I help you today?" is the product billing the user for its own ignorance. Specification is work, and most people are bad at it the way they are bad at writing legal contracts: they know what they want when they see it.

Draft-first

Do not ask what the email should say. Write it, and let them edit. Do not ask for preferences. Infer a default, show it, and make changing it one gesture. Proposals over questions, always. The user's job should be judgment, not authorship.

Watch the workaround

When a user does the same three steps for the third time, that is a specification written in behavior, clearer than anything they would type. "You have done this three times - should it just happen?" is the most respectful sentence an interface can produce, because it proves someone was paying attention.

Corrections are curriculum

Every edit a user makes to a draft is labeled training data, and it arrives with a name on it. Let them promote a correction into a standing rule or dismiss it as one-off. An interface that visibly improves - "I stopped doing the thing you kept fixing" - earns the next correction, and the next, until the defaults fit like a glove.
